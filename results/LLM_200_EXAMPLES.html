Ranking 200 candidates: worked examples

Section B4 of the extended-lists results page reports that GPT-6 Luna, given up to 200 followed account names, ranks 200 candidates better than SocialVec. These six users show what that looks like: three where it went well and three where it went badly, each rerun with the model asked to explain its choices.

1How the examples were chosen

  • Eligible users: those following at least 10 of the 200 candidates, so that a high AP cannot come from one lucky guess; at most one user per category.
  • Good: the largest AP above a random ranking, among answers that ranked at least 95% of the candidates.
  • Bad: the lowest AP, whatever the answer's completeness.
  • Rerun: the same prompt, followed accounts, shuffled candidates and reasoning effort (medium), with three output fields instead of one: the user's interests, the ranked list, and a one-sentence reason for each of the top 10. The chips show the AP of the original run (the one in Section B4) and of the rerun, which is a new call and can rank differently.
  • Caveat: the reasons are written by the model alongside its answer. They are its account of the ranking, not a record of the hidden reasoning, which the API does not return.
  • Privacy: users are identified by internal row only. Followed and candidate accounts are public accounts in SocialVec.

Across the three good examples, 26 of the 30 top-10 places in the original runs went to accounts the user follows (26 in the reruns); across the bad ones, 7 (8 in the reruns).

4What the examples show

  • When it works, the ranking follows a clear reading of the user. The model names an interest that the followed accounts support, such as political news (good example 1), esports and NFL (good example 2) or politically outspoken entertainers (good example 3), and puts the matching candidates first. 59 of the 60 reasons cite at least one account that is actually in the prompt (in bold).
  • The failures have different causes.
    • A plausible but wrong reading (bad example 1): following the University of Alabama leads the model to rank Alabama and college football first, while the user follows mostly professional teams. SocialVec scores 0.260 here.
    • A cut-off answer (bad example 2): the original answer ranked only 23 of 200 candidates. Its top picks are reasonable, but the relevant candidates it never listed count as misses.
    • Interests outside the category (bad example 3): the model favours actors known for activism, matching the user's political follows, over the mainstream actors the user follows. SocialVec does even worse here (0.066).
  • Single users vary a lot between runs. Repeating the identical prompt changed AP by 0.139 on average across these six users (from 0.692 to 0.252 for good example 3). The examples were chosen for extreme AP, so some move back toward the middle on a repeat. Section B4's comparison averages over 270 users and is the better guide to how well the method works; these examples show how it works.
