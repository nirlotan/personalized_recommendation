Results that use the extended category lists

These experiments use category lists of up to 1,000 accounts built from Wikidata and DBpedia, without an LLM and without manual validation (see results/EXTENDED_CATEGORIES_REPORT.md). They are kept here for reference and are not part of the main results. Section and table numbers refer to this page unless they name the main page.

B1Further down the category lists · Ranks 181–200

The original sheets stop at 100 entities per category. To go further, I repeated the ranks 81–100 experiments of the main page with the accounts ranked 181–200 in the extended lists.

  • Categories: the 10 categories whose extended list reaches 200 accounts.
  • Ranking: the extended lists rank accounts by SocialVec popularity, while ranks 81–100 were by Twitter follower count. The median candidate is the 6,595th most popular SocialVec entity, and 30% of the candidates lie beyond the top 10,000. Globally, these candidates are more popular than those of ranks 81–100 (Section B2): the lists differ, so a lower rank within a category does not mean a less popular account.
  • Profiles: user profiles exclude every account of the category's extended list (up to 1,000) and of its original sheet.
  • Popularity ranks worse than chance. At ranks 181–200 it scores 0.214 against 0.226 for a random ranking, and falls below chance in 6 of 10 categories.
  • Social similarity still personalizes well. It scores 0.470, +120% over popularity, significant in every category (p < 0.001), compared with +22% on the top 20 for the same users.
  • The same holds on all users. On 32,487 user–category pairs, the gain is +113%, vs. +20% on the top 20.
  • The LLM pattern repeats (Table B5).
    • No user information: GPT-6 Luna stays close to chance (0.265 vs. 0.227 for a random ranking).
    • 12 or 25 account names: it beats SocialVec on the same accounts (0.429 vs. 0.344 and 0.416 vs. 0.341).
    • 50 accounts: it scores about as well as SocialVec's full profile (0.535 vs. 0.498; p = 0.09, not significant).

Limitations

The extended lists are not manually validated. Known errors, such as people with acting credits listed as actors, team PR accounts and DBpedia mistypings, are described in the extended-lists report; they affect the candidate sets here. The LLM results use 30 users per category.

B2Global popularity of the candidate bands

Each band's position is set within its own category list. To compare bands across lists, every candidate is placed in the popularity ranking of all 201,346 SocialVec accounts (rank 1 is the most followed).

  • Ranks 181–200 are not less popular than ranks 81–100. Their median global rank is 8,098, against 13,672 for ranks 81–100 (Mann–Whitney p < 0.001), and their median follower count is 287,582 against 165,190. They are globally more popular in 7 of the 9 categories that have both bands.
  • Why: the original sheets hold 100 manually validated accounts per category, while the extended lists hold up to 1,000 accounts typed by Wikidata and DBpedia. Many popular accounts are missing from the original sheets (only 44–85 of each sheet's 100 fall in the extended top 200), so position 181 in an extended list can be more popular than position 81 in an original sheet. Because the extended lists are sorted by this same global rank, each category's 181–200 band is a narrow slice of it, either entirely inside or entirely outside the top 10,000.

B3Demographics-only LLM ranking, ranks 181–200

The shuffled-candidate rankings of Section 5 of the main page, repeated on ranks 181–200 of the extended lists for the 155 users who follow at least one of those candidates, with GPT-4o's descriptions. The method is described in the demographics report, which covers ranks 81–100 only.

  • Demographics help: GPT-4o's descriptions score 0.426 against 0.313 without user information (p < 0.001); its demographics-only descriptions 0.422 vs. 0.317.
  • Account names: 12 names score 0.446, not significantly above GPT-4o's descriptions (p = 0.198). 50 names score 0.535 against 0.456 for SocialVec's full profile (p = 0.002).

B4Ranking all 200 candidates

Does the LLM struggle with longer prompts? Here it must rank 200 candidates per category instead of 20. Worked examples are in a separate report.

  • Candidates: ranks 1–200 of the extended category lists (by SocialVec popularity), for the 9 categories that reach 200 accounts and have sampled users; 199.9 candidates per category on average, as entities without a SocialVec embedding are dropped.
  • Users: 270 of the users of Table 9 in the main page (its first 30 per category), all of whom follow at least one candidate (32.5 on average).
  • LLM: GPT-6 Luna at reasoning effort medium, with the candidates in one random order per user shared by both prompts. No user information uses the baseline prompt. Account names lists up to 200 accounts the user follows (197 on average), sampled among accounts with a known name, with every account of the category's extended list and original sheet removed.
  • A stronger prompt: with the authors' prompts at effort low, a pilot returned only 20–50 of the 200 ids when given 200 names, cutting the list off itself. Both prompts therefore add an explicit instruction to return every candidate exactly once.
  • Scoring incomplete answers: only the candidates the model actually ranked count. A relevant candidate it left out is a miss: it adds nothing to AP but stays in the denominator. Ids that are not candidates are dropped.
  • SocialVec: on the same accounts as the LLM's prompt, and on all followed entities (category accounts removed). Popularity ranks by follower count.
  • With 200 candidates, the LLM keeps a small lead. Given account names it scores 0.470, against 0.444 for SocialVec on the same accounts (p = 0.015) and 0.444 on all followed entities (p = 0.017). It is ahead in 6 of 9 categories. Placing the left-out candidates at the end in random order instead would give it 0.485, overstating the lead.
  • Without user information it is no better than popularity (0.323 vs. 0.324, p = 0.770), though far above chance (0.183).
  • Long lists are harder to complete (Table B7).
    • Coverage: even with the explicit instruction, only 10% of the name-based answers list every candidate, and 55% list at least 95% of them (19% and 71% without user information). Across users, 8% of the relevant candidates are missing from the name-based answers.
    • Short answers fall behind: for the 39 users whose answer stops before 60% of the candidates, the LLM scores 0.382 against 0.397 for SocialVec on the same accounts.
    • Complete answers: on the 104 users whose two answers are both at least 95% complete, the lead is 0.489 vs. 0.461, not significant (p = 0.096).
  • On the hardest candidates, the LLM does worse in the longer list (Table B9). Cutting each 200-candidate ranking down to the ranks 181–200 candidates allows a comparison with the 20-candidate prompts of Section B3, on the 155 users who follow one of them; both are scored with the rule above.
    • With names: the LLM drops from 0.535 to 0.472 (p = 0.004), although it now sees up to 200 names instead of 50.
    • Without user information: it drops from 0.313 to 0.266 (p = 0.007).
    • SocialVec does not depend on the list length (0.456 vs. 0.454). The LLM's lead over it on these candidates shrinks from +0.079 (p = 0.002) to +0.018 (p = 0.764).

How many candidates did the LLM rank?

Answers often contain about 200 ids, but some are repeats, so fewer distinct candidates are ranked. Only the first position of a repeated id counts, and ids that are not candidates are dropped. The number ranked does not depend on the other prompt's answer for the same user (correlation -0.06) or on the number of names listed (0.04).
